# Combinatorics - Axes, Major Domains, Subdomains, and Atomic Elements

## Scope

This note is a **best-effort maximal working taxonomy** of combinatorics.
It is meant to be mentally usable, not merely encyclopedic.
No finite list can literally guarantee every niche or newly named microfield, but this hierarchy is designed to capture the major recognized branches, their common subdomains, and the atomic building blocks that recur across them.

---

## I. Core Axes

These are the most useful first-order directions of combinatorics.

1. **Counting and generation**
   How many discrete objects exist, how they are built, and how they are encoded.
2. **Structure and classification**
   What kinds of discrete objects exist and how they are organized.
3. **Extremes, bounds, and unavoidable patterns**
   What is maximal, minimal, forbidden, or inevitable.
4. **Randomness and typical behavior**
   What discrete objects usually look like under probabilistic models.
5. **Algebraic and representation-theoretic interaction**
   How symmetry, linear algebra, polynomials, and representation theory govern discrete objects.
6. **Geometric and topological interaction**
   How combinatorics interfaces with shape, space, incidence, convexity, and topological invariants.
7. **Algorithmic, computational, and optimization interaction**
   How to construct, search, optimize, sample, or decide discrete structures efficiently.
8. **Arithmetic and number-theoretic interaction**
   How additive, multiplicative, and arithmetic structure manifests combinatorially.
9. **Infinite and logical interaction**
   How combinatorial ideas extend to infinite sets, infinitary partition phenomena, and logic-adjacent discrete structure.

## VI. Mental Compression

If the field is compressed aggressively, combinatorics can be viewed as:

**discrete objects + discrete constraints + transformations + counting + inevitability + randomness + optimization**

Or, more operationally:

1. Choose a class of discrete objects.
2. Ask how many exist.
3. Ask what structure they admit.
4. Ask what must or cannot happen.
5. Ask what is typical at random.
6. Ask what algebraic, geometric, or topological machinery reveals.
7. Ask how to construct, optimize, or decide efficiently.
