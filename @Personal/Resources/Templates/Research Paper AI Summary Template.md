## **Opening perspective**

Open in a natural, paragraph-based way that orients the reader.

Explain:
- what kind of problem space this paper belongs to,
- what kind of contribution it appears to make,
- and why someone serious about the field would care about it.

Do NOT use stiff phrasing like:
- “this paper aims to…”
- “the objective of this paper is…”

This should feel like a knowledgeable person introducing the paper before diving into details.

## **Full walkthrough and explanation**

Write the main body as a **continuous, deeply explanatory walkthrough** of the paper.

This is the core of the template.

### **How this should feel**

- Like a **serious teaching conversation**
- Like someone **walking through the paper with you**
- Like ideas are **building on each other naturally**

NOT:
- a checklist
- a form
- disconnected sections

---

### **Structure rules (VERY IMPORTANT)**

- Use **bolded section headers** to separate *major conceptual regions*
- Use **subheadings when needed** to separate:
  - major ideas
  - mechanisms
  - stages
  - components
  - distinctions
- Keep the writing **primarily paragraph-based**
- Use bullet points **only when they genuinely improve clarity or retention**
- Use Numbered lists when it benifits the reader 

---

### **DO NOT create rigid sections like:**

- What kind of paper this is  
- Background and context  
- Core ideas  
- Evidence, experiments, proofs  
- Important relationships  
- What is easy to misunderstand  
- Why this paper matters  
- Limitations  

These are **NOT headings**.

They are **conceptual responsibilities** that must be covered **naturally inside the explanation**.

---

### **What MUST be covered (implicitly, inside the flow)**

As you explain the paper, you must naturally include:

- what type of paper it is and how it should be read
- the background and context it emerges from
- the core ideas and conceptual framework
- all important objects, variables, structures, or components
- how the method / system / argument unfolds
- the main claims, findings, or results
- the evidence or reasoning supporting those claims
- important relationships and distinctions between concepts
- points that are easy to misunderstand or misinterpret
- places where the paper is incomplete, unclear, outdated, overstated, or wrong
- what the better-supported or actually correct view is when that happens
- why the paper matters (implicitly explained, not labeled)
- limitations, caveats, and open questions
- how it connects to other work or ideas

Do **not** explicitly label these.  
They should emerge **naturally in the explanation**.

---

### **Truth-checking and correction behavior**

- Do **not** assume the paper is correct just because it is published or influential
- If the paper gets something materially wrong, say so **in the main walkthrough at the exact point where it comes up**
- Immediately explain what is more accurate, better established, or actually true in reality
- If the paper leaves out an important condition, dependency, mechanism, comparison, or limitation, state what is missing and why that missing context matters
- If the paper is ambiguous rather than clearly false, say that the claim is underspecified, weakly supported, or not fully clarified
- Present these corrections in clean prose as part of the teaching flow
- Do **not** hide them in parentheses
- Do **not** create a separate "what the paper got wrong" section
- When useful, distinguish between:
  - a historically understandable simplification
  - an actually inaccurate claim
  - a claim that is directionally useful but incomplete

The goal is not to attack the paper.  
The goal is to teach the paper **and** reality at the same time.

---

### **Depth and fidelity requirement**

This is NOT a summary.

This is a **high-fidelity reconstruction of the paper for real understanding**.

You must:

- include ALL important concepts mentioned in the paper
- preserve terminology used in the paper (while explaining it)
- retain mechanisms, distinctions, and arguments
- stay faithful to what the paper says without silently inheriting its mistakes
- avoid replacing content with vague abstraction
- avoid skipping “less interesting” sections

If unsure, **include more detail, not less**.

---

### **Handling technical content**

When the paper introduces:
- equations
- variables
- graph structures
- model components
- datasets
- system modules

Explain them immediately and in context.

If an equation appears:

$$
{{equation}}
$$

Then explain:
- what each symbol represents
- what the equation is doing
- how it fits into the system
- why it matters

Never leave math unexplained.

---

### **Pipeline / process formatting**

Whenever the paper contains a sequence or flow, represent it like:

Stage 1 → Stage 2 → Stage 3 → Output

Examples:
- Input → Weighted Sum → Threshold → Update
- Data → Embedding → Message Passing → Prediction

Rules:
- Always include the pipeline when a process exists
- Then explain it in natural paragraph form
- Do NOT break into rigid input/output labels
- If multiple processes exist → include multiple pipelines

---

### **Terminology handling**

- Use terminology from the paper when appropriate
- Do NOT assume the reader understands it
- Explain higher-level or abstract terms as they appear

---

### **Goal of the walkthrough**

The reader should:
- understand what the paper actually says
- understand how it works
- understand how its pieces connect
- understand why it behaves the way it does

This is about **real cognitive understanding**, not recognition.

## **Subtle points, clarifications, and limits**

After the main walkthrough, include a short section that clarifies any **leftover** subtleties that were not already handled naturally earlier.

This is **not** where you dump factual corrections.  
If the paper gets something wrong, overstates a claim, or leaves out necessary context, address that **inside the walkthrough when it first becomes relevant**.

This short section can still clarify:

- where misunderstandings commonly happen
- what the paper does NOT claim
- where assumptions are hidden
- where limitations exist
- where conclusions should not be overextended

Keep this tight but insightful.
